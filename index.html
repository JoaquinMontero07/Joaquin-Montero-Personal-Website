Where I'm from

Background

My name is Joaquin Montero, and I'm currently a sophomore at Duke University. I was born in Argentina, to Argentinian parents, and moved to Chile when I was two and a half; I always include that half a year, because it stretches out the time I can say I actually lived home.

What was supposed to be two or three years in Chile became fifteen. I grew up there, built a life there, and I'm genuinely grateful for it, but I never stopped identifying as Argentinian. It took me a while to understand why. The clearest answer came from watching how differently people connect in each place: Argentina's culture is communal in a way that's hard to describe until you've felt it. People don't just know each other, they treat each other like family. I think of my parents' friends still at our dining table at 3am, cards scattered everywhere, nobody checking the time, and remember watching from the hallway, wanting that to be mine someday. Chile, by contrast, is more individualistic, more compartmentalized. I grew up inside that contrast, and eventually realized that the belonging I was chasing wasn't tied to a place at all. It was something I could build wherever I was.

Numbers have always made sense to me in a way other things didn't, which is why I paired Math with Economics at Duke. Growing up close to Argentina meant that side of me never stayed purely theoretical. Inflation, currency instability, and capital controls weren't abstract to me, that was the economic environment I grew up watching. I found myself drawn to understanding it rather than just living around it, which is a large part of why markets, and specifically fixed income, attract me in the way they do.

Work Experience

Over the summer, I completed formal training in bond pricing, duration and convexity, and option valuation, then put it to use writing weekly and monthly reports on Argentina's macroeconomy for internal distribution. I also built an Excel-based bond calculator that could value four different bond types across a portfolio of thirty plus bonds from a single input.

The larger project was a model predicting Uruguay's central bank rate decisions. I automated the daily download of the central bank's yield vectors with VBA, filtered them to peso-denominated letras, and fit a Nelson-Siegel curve to each day's data, which gave me two curve-based features to work with. Those fed a two-stage model: one stage deciding whether the rate changes at all, and a second deciding direction only when the first predicts a change. It was validated walk-forward, retrained at every meeting using only prior data.

Most committee meetings end in a hold, so the problem was not solely optimizing overall accuracy. What mattered was whether the model caught the meetings where the rate actually changed. I tested decision thresholds from 0.30 to 0.70 and picked one that scores slightly lower on overall accuracy than the alternatives; however, it was able to catch meaningfully more of the rate changes than the rest.

I worked front office, supporting capital markets and retail investment operations for around forty clients. Most of my time went to executing client orders, buy and sell, in both equities and fixed income on the Argentine stock exchange. I also reviewed trade details before execution, making sure everything matched client instructions exactly, which taught me fast how little room for error there actually is. It was my first real exposure to a trading floor, and the pace of it, the pressure to move fast and stay accurate, genuinely attracted me.

I worked on a research project studying how ChatGPT-4 could help simplify abstract language in classrooms across eight grade levels. My work involved designing and running interviews with over ten teachers, walking them through how to use the tool in their own lessons, then going back through more than thirty rounds of feedback to see what actually made a lesson clearer or harder to follow. I ended up writing a section of the final analysis on how abstract language affects learning. I understood the importance of clarity when communicating in a learning environment, being able to explain a complicated topic in simple, understandable words, which is something I still think about every time I write something of my own.

Looking ahead

Where I want to end up is still an open question. Finance is broad, and different parts of it pull at me for different reasons. But there's one thread I keep coming back to.

I grew up close to a version of financial volatility most people only study from the outside: unstable currencies and environments where nothing about stability was assumed. I saw what that does to a country and the people living in it. What I've never gotten to see is the other side of that: how companies actually work through that kind of pressure, not just how they end up in it. That's part of what draws me toward corners of finance like restructuring and distressed credit.

Longer term, I'd like to work in emerging markets, and Latin America in particular, which would also mean going back home to my friends and family. It's also a region with substantial room to grow, still developing in ways that more established markets aren't. It's what I know, and it's what I'm most curious about.
